Mangrove Deforestation Analysis — Ambanja and Ambaro Bays, Madagascar

📍 Madagascar   📅 2025

Mangroves are disappearing. Can spatial analysis reveal where, when, and why?

The Problem

One of the world’s most valuable ecosystems — vanishing fast

Mangrove forests are among the most carbon-rich and ecologically productive ecosystems on the planet. They protect coastlines, support fisheries, and store significant quantities of carbon. Yet they are disappearing rapidly due to agricultural expansion, timber harvesting, and coastal development.

This project quantifies mangrove loss across administrative boundaries in the Ambanja and Ambaro Bays of Madagascar between 1999 and 2019, and investigates whether deforestation is spatially associated with proximity to paths and permanent rivers.

The Data

A global tidal wetland dataset from Google Earth Engine

The Murray Global Tidal Wetland Change dataset was downloaded from Google Earth Engine, capturing changes in tidal wetland extent globally at high spatial resolution. Administrative boundary layers — communes and fokontany — and infrastructure layers including paths and permanent rivers were used for spatial analysis.

Step 1 — Data Cleaning

Removing noise before analysis

The Murray raster was converted to polygon format using arcpy.conversion.RasterToPolygon(). ArcPy cursors were then used to remove null value polygons (coded as 0) and rename the gridcode field to “loss_year”:

arcpy.management.AlterField(mangrove_polygon_name, 'gridcode', 'loss_year')

with arcpy.da.UpdateCursor(mangrove_polygon_name, 'loss_year') as cursor:
    for row in cursor:
        if row[0] == 0:
            cursor.deleteRow()

Step 2 — Quantifying Loss by Administrative Unit

Which communes and fokontany lost the most?

The cleaned polygons were spatially joined to commune and fokontany boundary layers. Area was calculated using arcpy.management.CalculateGeometryAttributes(), and arcpy.analysis.Statistics() summarized total loss area (km²) and average loss year by administrative unit. Results were exported to Excel using Pandas.

Figure 1. Total mangrove loss (km²) by commune, 1999–2019. Antsakoamanondro recorded the highest loss at 3.05 km², followed by Anorontsangana (1.86 km²) and Maherivaratra (1.67 km²).

Figure 2. Top 10 fokontany by total mangrove loss. Ambodimanga Sud (1.47 km²) and Andrahibo (1.30 km²) recorded the highest losses at the sub-commune level.

Figure 3. Average year of mangrove loss by commune. Darker bars indicate more recent loss (2015 and later).

Step 3 — Proximity Analysis

Does accessibility drive deforestation?

Multi-ring buffers of 1km, 2km, and 5km were applied around paths and permanent rivers using ArcPy’s Buffer tool, then intersected with mangrove loss polygons to quantify deforestation within each distance band.

Figure 4. Total mangrove loss by distance from paths. Loss increases with distance, suggesting remoteness — not accessibility — may be a stronger driver of deforestation.

Results

Where loss is greatest — and what drives it

The analysis revealed that most loss occurred between 2012 and 2015. At the commune level, Antsakoamanondro experienced the greatest loss (~3.05 km²), while Ambodimanga Sud led at the fokontany level (~1.47 km²).

Zone Area
Most affected commune Antsakoamanondro (~3.05 km²)
Most affected fokontany Ambodimanga Sud (~1.47 km²)
Loss within 1km of paths 0.66 km²
Loss within 5km of paths 7.71 km²

Project Significance

This project illustrates how Python-based spatial analysis can automate repetitive geoprocessing steps, handle large datasets systematically, and produce structured outputs that directly inform conservation planning. The workflow is transferable to any region where tidal wetland change data is available — making it a scalable approach for monitoring mangrove loss globally.
